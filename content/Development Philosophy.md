# principles
## do it (mostly) right the first time
i believe there is a delicate point to be made here: "easy wins" and "low-hanging fruit" are important endeavors that quickly bring value to stakeholders. this, in turn, can bring value back to the data team in the form of trust and partnership from other departments. but i fear some developers view such projects as permission to shoot from the hip. keeping discipline, thoroughly researching business needs and best practices, and carefully developing to the best of your abilities will assure the "easy win" doesn't turn into a mess of code that will have you checking over your shoulder before you open it for maintenance. of course, the balance needs to be struck between speed of delivery and the time spent on solution design. but i believe taking a realistic approach will naturally point a developer toward a more grounded and resilient solution. remember scope creep tendencies - there's a good chance your low-hanging fruit is going to immediately lead to a request for low-hanging-fruit pie. 
## iteration
the (mostly) from the previous blurb. you will not think of every possible scenario and pitfall before you begin solution design. it is far more important to start development and allow space for refinement over time than it is to draft or brainstorm your way into development hell. the great part about iterative development is that your personal skills develop faster as well. something you learned in iteration 3 of project #1 may graduate to initial solution design of project #2. 
## design with humans in mind
[[modular programming]]'s star is rising in data engineering thanks to tools like dbt macros and pipeline parameterizing in fabric. the goal is valuable: tear down monolith solutions in favor of smaller, easier to maintain modules of code. i have personally participated in this growing principle and it's power is easy to see. i've also shown new developers this modular code, and it is equally easy to see their eyes glaze over when you see dynamic content where a nice user interface dropdown menu used to be. again, a balance must be struck. are you sacrificing a developer's ability to follow and debug processes for modularity? or, are you sacrificing the legitimate power of modular design for gentle learning curves? i do not believe legibility and modularity are mutually exclusive, and designing with humans in mind will, hopefully, keep that balance struck.
